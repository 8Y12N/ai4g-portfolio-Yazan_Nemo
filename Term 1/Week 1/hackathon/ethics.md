## Ethical Reflection

**Who is excluded?**
The app assumes fluent English and comfort with classical philosophical language and citations. This excludes non-native speakers, younger students, and anyone who finds argumentative, text-based dialogue intimidating rather than engaging. It also assumes a device with a modern browser and a stable connection, excluding users with older devices or limited data access.

**What does it assume about the learner?**
It assumes the user is willing to write out and defend a personal position, then have it challenged. Someone looking for a quick, correct answer, or a supportive tone, would likely find the repeated counter-examples frustrating rather than useful. It also assumes a baseline comfort with ambiguity, since the app deliberately avoids telling the user what is right or wrong and instead only points out contradictions in their own words.

**How could this be used for the wrong reasons?**
Because Socrates is designed to find and press on weak points in the user's stated position, it could be used to make someone doubt a reasonable belief just to "win" the exchange rather than to build genuine understanding. Users prone to anxiety or overthinking could experience the repeated exposure of contradictions as personal failure rather than as productive doubt. There is also a risk that users treat the AI's counter-examples as authoritative simply because it is a computer, rather than continuing to question it themselves, which defeats the purpose of the method.

**What has been done to reduce this risk?**
The app frames confusion explicitly as progress, not failure ("it's okay to doubt"), and the AI is designed to only point out contradictions rather than state what the "right" answer is, keeping the user in control of their own conclusions. Session length is also kept short (3-4 exchanges per question, about 5 minutes total), limiting how far the pressure can build in a single sitting.
