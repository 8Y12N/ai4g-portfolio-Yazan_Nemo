## Ethical Reflection

**Who is excluded?**
The app assumes fluent, comfortable English and familiarity with abstract philosophical language. This leaves out non-native speakers, younger students, and anyone who finds text-heavy, argumentative dialogue intimidating rather than engaging. It also assumes a device with a modern browser and a stable connection, so it excludes users on older phones or with limited data access.

**What does it assume about the learner?**
It assumes the user already has some interest in philosophy and is willing to be challenged and shown they might be wrong. Someone looking for a quick answer or a supportive, encouraging tone would likely find the Socratic pushback frustrating rather than useful. It also assumes the user can read and reflect on their own reasoning in writing, which is not a universal skill or comfort level.

**How could this be used for the wrong reasons?**
Because the AI is designed to dismantle the user's stated position through counter-examples, it could be used to make someone doubt a genuinely reasonable belief just to "win" the exchange, rather than to build understanding. If a user is already anxious or prone to overthinking, being repeatedly told their reasoning has a contradiction could reinforce self-doubt instead of critical thinking. There is also a risk that users treat Socrates' conclusions as authoritative simply because it is an AI, instead of continuing to question it the way the tool intends.

**What would reduce this risk?**
Making it clear upfront that the goal is practicing reasoning, not reaching a "correct" answer, and giving users an easy way to exit or disagree with the AI's framing without it being treated as a failure.
